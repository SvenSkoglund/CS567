\section{Related Work}
Our sandbox sat at the intersection of four lines of research: immersive virtual
reality (VR) for science learning, the difficulty of rendering weight and force on
commodity hardware, the pseudo-haptic techniques that stood in for that missing
sensation, and the interaction-design foundations that made direct manipulation
legible. We reviewed each in turn and closed by stating what distinguished our
approach.

\subsection{Immersive VR for Science Learning}
The pedagogical case for immersive VR rested less on visual fidelity than on the
sense of being present. Slater and S{\'a}nchez-Vives framed this through the place
illusion (the feeling of being in the virtual space) and the plausibility
illusion (the feeling that events were really happening), which together led
users to respond to virtual events as if they were real~\cite{slater2016enhancing}.
For a physics sandbox this mattered directly. If a learner treated a virtual block
and lever as real objects, then manipulating them could carry the intuitive weight
that a 2D simulation could not.

Empirical work in physics education added a consistent caveat: immersion helped,
but only when the learning experience was designed for it. A meta-analysis by Wu
et al.\ across many head-mounted display studies found that immersive VR yielded a
positive but moderate overall effect on learning performance, and that this effect
was strongly moderated by factors such as subject domain, learner control, and how
the experience was designed~\cite{wu2020effectiveness}. Tsivitanidou, Georgiou, and
Ioannou reported on an inquiry-based immersive VR physics activity from two angles:
the learning experience, in which they observed conceptual gains alongside an
interaction with learners' attitudinal profiles~\cite{tsivitanidou2021inquiry}, and
the design of that same experience, which they documented as a worked case of
learning-experience design~\cite{georgiou2021led}. We read this pair as evidence
that immersive VR physics learning was feasible and could produce conceptual gains;
it established that a VR physics activity could work, though it did not itself
isolate felt weight or the effect of a haptic cue. One common way to scaffold such
experiences was an embedded pedagogical agent. Makransky et al.\ studied one such
agent and found a gender-matching effect: a virtual instructor aided learning most
when its apparent gender matched the learner, a specific interaction rather than a
general endorsement of pedagogical agents~\cite{makransky2019gender}. Taken
together, these studies established that immersive physics learning was promising
but design-sensitive. The value came from what the learner was asked to do and how
the task was scaffolded, not from the headset itself. We took a deliberately
different scaffolding stance. Rather than an in-scene agent, we scaffolded through
scene and task design, using tightly scoped scenarios that isolated a single
variable so that the cause of each outcome was unambiguous.

\subsection{The Missing Channel: Rendering Weight and Force}
The central obstacle for a physics-of-materials experience was that the very
quantity the lessons depended on, namely weight and the force needed to move a
loaded mechanism, was the one commodity VR could not deliver. A handheld controller
weighed the same whatever it represented. Wang et al.\ surveyed the state of haptic
display for VR and made clear that genuine, general-purpose force feedback remained
far from consumer reach, constrained by actuation, workspace, and grounding
requirements that headset-and-controller setups did not
meet~\cite{wang2019haptic}. Their survey framed the design problem for us
precisely. To make a heavier object feel heavier on a Quest 2, we could not wait
for the hardware to render the force, and instead had to substitute perception for
physics.

\subsection{Pseudo-Haptics: Substituting Perception for Physics}
Pseudo-haptics addressed this gap by exploiting the dominance of vision over
proprioception. Manipulating the visual consequences of a motion, such as the
control-to-display gain or a small lag between hand and object, induced a felt
sense of weight, stiffness, or resistance without any force actuator. The
foundational demonstration came from L{\'e}cuyer et al., who showed that altering
the visual response of an isometric input device made users perceive force and
stiffness that no actuator produced, establishing that a control-to-display
manipulation alone could stand in for force feedback~\cite{lecuyer2000pseudo}.
Ujitoko and Ban surveyed the design space and applications of pseudo-haptics,
cataloguing how such visual manipulations reliably produced haptic sensations and
how they had been applied~\cite{ujitoko2021pseudo}. Their taxonomy was the
methodological backbone for how we mapped mass onto gain and visual lag.

Two results grounded our specific cue, the felt \emph{weight} of a held object.
Dominjon et al.\ manipulated the control/display ratio of a manipulated object in a
virtual environment and showed that this ratio directly changed its perceived mass,
so that the same physical motion could be made to feel heavier or lighter by scaling
its visual displacement~\cite{dominjon2005influence}. Rietzler et al.\ carried the
same idea to consumer VR: by introducing perceivable offsets between the tracked
hand and the rendered object, they elicited a reliable sensation of weight on a
commodity headset with no force hardware~\cite{rietzler2018breaking}. Together these
established that a gain or tracking-offset manipulation could render felt weight on
exactly the class of device we targeted, and they fixed the design variable for us:
the cue had to track an object's \emph{mass}, the quantity that determines weight,
rather than its density.

\subsection{Foundations of 3D Interaction}
Whatever the illusion, the learner first had to be able to reach out, grab, and
place objects fluidly, or the physics never got probed. We grounded our interaction
design in Bowman et al.'s canonical treatment of 3D user interfaces, which formalized
selection, manipulation, and travel as the core tasks of spatial interaction and
catalogued the techniques for each~\cite{bowman2004}. This foundation informed the
grab-and-place model at the heart of the sandbox and kept our contribution, the
pseudo-haptic property mapping, layered on top of well-understood manipulation
techniques rather than reinventing them.

\subsection{What Was Different About Our Approach}
Two gaps emerged from this literature. First, immersive physics studies consistently
delivered visual and interactive experiences but rarely gave learners a genuine felt
contrast in weight between objects. A student could see that two blocks differed, but
did not feel one resist more than the other. Second, pseudo-haptic studies
convincingly rendered the felt weight of an isolated
object~\cite{dominjon2005influence, rietzler2018breaking}, but seldom spanned both
material properties (density, buoyancy, friction) and simple machines such as the
lever within one coherent environment and interaction model. Our sandbox targeted
this intersection. We used pseudo-haptic gain and visual-lag modulation as a unifying
mechanism that made an unrenderable physical quantity perceptible across a family of
controlled, single-variable scenarios. Crucially, the cue tracked the \emph{mass} of
whatever was held, the quantity that actually determines weight, so that density was
taught where it belongs, through the volume difference between equal-mass blocks and
through float-or-sink behavior, rather than being conflated with heaviness. Because
our contribution was an interaction technique for making a physical quantity felt, we
evaluated it at two levels: a system level, checking that the cue behaved
monotonically with mass and that each scenario matched its closed-form prediction;
and a human level, a between-subjects study testing whether the weight cue changed how
accurately learners predicted outcomes that depended on mass, density, and load.
