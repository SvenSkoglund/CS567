\begin{abstract}
Physical intuition about mass, volume, density, and friction, and about simple
machines such as levers, is hard to build from equations and 2D diagrams, and the
demonstrations that would make it concrete are often impractical or unsafe in a
classroom. We built an immersive virtual-reality sandbox in which learners held,
compared, and tested blocks of different materials on a lever, in a water tank, and
on a ramp. Because commodity headsets cannot render true weight, we used pseudo-haptic
cues, modulating control-to-display gain and visual lag, so that a heavier object felt
heavier; the cue tracked mass, while density was revealed through the volume of
equal-mass blocks and through whether they floated or sank. The application was pure
VR with no in-scene agent; agentic AI was instead the development workflow. We
validated the system against closed-form physics and ran a between-subjects study
testing whether the weight cue improved how accurately learners predicted outcomes
that depended on mass, density, and load.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{3D user interfaces; virtual reality; embodied learning; pseudo-haptics;
physics education; agentic AI; AI-assisted development}

\section{Introduction and Motivation}
Introductory physics rests on relationships that students can state long before they
can predict with them. Density relates mass to volume, buoyancy determines whether an
object floats, friction resists sliding in proportion to the normal load, and simple
machines such as levers trade force for distance. The usual failure is not arithmetic
but intuition. Asked whether a kilogram of gold or a kilogram of ice is larger, which
of two blocks slides farther down a ramp, or where a load balances on a lever, many
learners fall back on misconceptions such as ``heavier means bigger.'' Textbooks and
2D simulations show the formula and the result, yet they do not let a student
\emph{feel} the difference.

Doing the demonstration in real life is harder than it sounds: the comparisons that
isolate a single variable are exactly the ones that are awkward, expensive, or unsafe
to stage, since matched-mass materials are not classroom stock and friction is hard
to hold constant. Virtual reality can present these controlled manipulations on
demand, but a handheld controller weighs the same whatever it represents, so the
sensation many lessons depend on is missing. The question we set out to answer was
whether a spatial interface could make physical properties and mechanical behavior
\emph{felt} well enough to matter, given that the dominant channel for weight and
force is the one current hardware cannot deliver.

\section{Approach}
We built a VR ``physics sandbox'' in which the learner probed scenarios and the
interface made the governing quantity perceptible. The scenarios targeted material
properties and one mechanical device: the learner compared blocks by lifting them,
put them in water to see buoyancy, released them on a ramp to compare friction, and
placed them on a lever to find its balance point. Density was made visible rather than
felt: two \emph{equal-mass} blocks of different materials differed in size, so the
denser one was visibly smaller, and the same materials floated or sank by density in
the tank. In each case a single variable was isolated so the outcome had a clear
cause. Weight was conveyed through pseudo-haptics, modulating control-to-display gain
and visual lag so that a heavier block felt
heavier~\cite{ujitoko2021pseudo, dominjon2005influence, rietzler2018breaking}. The cue
tracked an object's \emph{mass}, the quantity that determines its weight, rather than
its density: equal-mass blocks weigh the same whatever their material, so making a
denser block feel heavier would have taught the very ``heavier means bigger''
misconception the sandbox set out to correct. This is where the project complied with
HCI research in \textbf{Virtual Reality}: the contribution was an interaction technique
for making an unrenderable physical quantity perceptible through direct, embodied
manipulation.

The second aspect was the \textbf{agentic-AI} development workflow, and we were
deliberate about the distinction the assignment drew. The finished application had no
in-scene chatbot for the learner to talk to. Instead, agentic AI was how the software
was built: an AI coding agent worked in the project with real tools, editing Unity
C\# scripts, compiling, entering Play mode, reading back measurements and test output,
and iterating toward a working scene over many steps rather than emitting one block of
code from a single prompt. We reported what it implemented, where it needed human
correction, and how the workflow shaped the build. The professor confirmed that a pure
VR project built this way satisfied the assignment.

\section{System}
\label{sec:system}
The sandbox was a first-person VR scene with a workbench and a side table (Figures
\ref{fig:mockup} through \ref{fig:prototype}). It held four materials (pine, ice,
aluminum, and steel) in two block sets: a same-mass set of 1\,kg cubes, in which size
alone showed density, and a same-size set of one-litre cubes from 0.5 to 7.85\,kg, in
which only the weight cue told them apart. The learner grabbed objects with Quest
Touch controllers. A lever with centimetre marks reported the torque on each side, a
water tank floated or sank blocks by Archimedes' principle, and a 12.5$^\circ$ ramp
with a start line let friction decide which blocks slid. A side panel showed each
object's properties and the measured outcome of an interaction when the sandbox was
used freely; in experiment mode it was hidden and replaced by the panel in
Figure~\ref{fig:experiment}.

We built in \textbf{Unity 6} with the \textbf{XR Interaction Toolkit} and
\textbf{OpenXR}, in \textbf{C\#}. The scene ran from a PC on a \textbf{Meta Quest 2}
over Quest Link. Physics used Unity's rigid-body engine with per-material density and
friction, and the entire scene was generated from code so that its layout was
reproducible.

\textbf{The weight cue.} While a block was held, the object was displayed at a position
derived from the hand's. Gain fell from 1 at 0.25\,kg to 0.35 at 10\,kg and lag rose
from 0 to 300\,ms, both on a logarithmic scale of mass, so that equal mass ratios gave
equal cue steps. Early versions applied gain in every direction, as earlier
work described~\cite{dominjon2005influence}, and that proved wrong in practice: after a
sideways move the block stayed offset sideways, as if pulled by sideways gravity, which
the author noticed within seconds of watching it. Only \emph{upward} displacement was
therefore scaled, so that a heavy block hung below the hand and came level again when
lowered. Lag was limited the same way: a heavy block that trailed the hand on the way down
looked as if it were floating, so a block above where the hand placed it fell with only
15\,ms of smoothing, whatever its mass. A plain gain also let a heavy block drift arbitrarily far from the hand on a
long lift, so the shortfall levelled off with height, at about 15\,cm for the heaviest
block. Held objects were kinematic, and each was handed back to the physics engine on
release, so the simulation and the cue did not fight.

\textbf{Departures from the proposal.} The cue applied to held objects only; because
the plank was not grasped, a loaded lever had no cue of its own, and its behavior was
shown by its tilt. The lever's hinge received a light centering spring, because loads
resting on top of a pivot are an unstable equilibrium; it behaved like a beam balance.
Two separate builds proved unnecessary, since a single build could set the cue from the
condition. The questions moved from a laptop into the headset so that one log captured
everything, and a standalone Quest build and hand tracking were set aside in favor of
Quest Link and controllers.

\section{Prototype Validation}
\label{sec:validation}
Before any human data was collected, the prototype had to be shown to behave correctly,
as a precondition for a meaningful study. \textbf{Physics fidelity:} scripted checks
compared each scenario against its closed-form prediction with a 5\% tolerance.
Submerged fractions matched density ratios (pine 0.500, ice 0.917, with aluminum and
steel sinking). The lever balanced when the torques matched (1.88 against 1.80\,N$\cdot$m,
settling at $-0.2^\circ$). On the ramp, each material was released from rest at three
slopes (Table~\ref{tab:ramp}). Blocks stayed put when the slope's tangent was below the
static friction and otherwise accelerated within 3.1\% of $g(\sin\theta -
\mu_d\cos\theta)$; pine and steel, with equal friction and a nearly sixteen-fold mass
difference, gave the same result.

\textbf{Pseudo-haptic behavior:} unit tests swept the full mass range and confirmed that
gain never rose and lag never fell with mass, that displayed lift never rose with mass,
and that the offset stayed bounded. In Play mode, a 30\,cm hand lift left a 7.85\,kg
block 12.7\,cm below the hand and a 0.5\,kg block 2.5\,cm below it, and a further 50\,cm
sideways move left both offsets unchanged. \textbf{Experiment mode:} unit tests confirmed that twelve
participants filled the two conditions equally and that the heaviness trials were balanced
across sides, and a scripted participant completed full sessions through the real grab
and button pipeline, 24 responses and 16 grabs per session with no errors, its data marked
as a self-test. \textbf{Performance:} because
dropped frames would confound the cue, each session logged the mean frame rate of each
block and the number of frames over a 72\,Hz budget.

\textbf{Development workflow:} because agentic AI was our build method, we reported which

where human correction was needed, drawn from a task log kept from the first day of
development. The checks also caught real defects: the ramp check showed that the physics
engine's default friction model held blocks at slopes where the closed form said they
should slide, and enabling its improved patch friction brought the results within
tolerance. Passing these checks established that the two conditions differed only in the
weight cue, which was what the study in Section~\ref{sec:methodology} required.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
Making a physical quantity \emph{felt} was only worthwhile if it changed what learners
understood, so beyond validating the system we conducted a controlled study with human
participants. It asked one question: did a pseudo-haptic weight cue, added to direct
manipulation in VR, change how accurately learners predicted outcomes that depended on
mass, density, and load, compared with the same manipulation without the cue? We report
the study using the standard structure of participants, apparatus, procedure, and design.

\subsection{Participants}
Twelve participants, six in each condition, were recruited
from non-physics courses, targeting students who had not taken university physics so
that measurable misconceptions were present to begin with. Recruitment used the standard
class data-collection form. The project was not intended for outside publication, so no
IRB submission was required; had publication been pursued later, an IRB with a faculty
principal investigator would have been arranged before any recruitment began.
Participation took about 15 minutes, and participants could stop at any time. Participants
were identified only by a two-digit ID.

\subsection{Apparatus}
The study used a Meta Quest~2 over Quest Link, running the sandbox of
Section~\ref{sec:system} in the prototype's experiment mode. The same build served both
conditions. With the cue on, gain and lag tracked the held object's mass; with the cue
off, objects followed the hand one to one. The panel of readings and the debug line
between hand and object were hidden, so that the two conditions looked identical except
for how objects moved. Participants read and answered on a panel in the scene, pointing a
controller at a button and squeezing the grip or pulling the trigger; the grip was also
how they grabbed blocks. The experimenter's desktop showed the current condition, which
was not drawn in the headset. Before any session, the prototype passed the validation
checks of Section~\ref{sec:validation}.

Every session wrote three files, flushed after each row so that a crash lost nothing: a
responses file with one row per answer or task (choice, correctness, response time,
completion time, falls, and task measures), an interactions file with one row per grab
(mass, hold time, hand lift, peak offset, gain, and lag), and a session file with the order,
device, and frame rates. Pilot and self-test runs were marked as such in each row and
excluded from analysis.

\subsection{Procedure}
The experimenter entered the participant's ID, which set the condition order. Each
participant then completed a short training scene to learn pointing and grab-and-place,
followed by one sequence under a single cue setting: a four-item pre-test; four heaviness
trials; the four scenes of Table~\ref{tab:scenes}; the post-test; and a five-item
questionnaire. Participants stood at a station for each task and were moved
between stations automatically, so no walking or joystick travel was involved. In each
scene they first committed to a prediction, then carried out the task, then saw the
outcome. A heaviness trial presented two identical, unlabeled 10\,cm purple cubes of different mass,
in a color matching none of the sandbox's materials so that it implied no density.
Participants lifted both and chose the left block, the right block, or that they felt the
same, with four mass pairs differing by a factor of four to sixteen and the heavier block on
each side equally often. The session ended with a brief interview about how handling the
blocks felt.

\subsection{Design}
The experiment was between subjects with one independent variable, the \emph{weight cue}
(on vs.\ off). Each participant used one setting for the whole session, assigned from the
participant ID so that twelve participants gave six in each condition.

The original design was within subjects, with every participant completing a full block in
each condition. Pilot runs showed that this was too convoluted, making the session long and
repetitive for participants. We moved to the present design so that the study could focus
more closely on the research question, accepting that comparing separate groups requires
more participants to detect an effect of the same size.

The primary dependent variable was \emph{prediction accuracy} on a short misconception test
given before and after the session's activities. Its four items
followed the style of the Force Concept Inventory~\cite{hestenes1992force}: which of two
equal-mass blocks is larger, which floats, which slides farther down a ramp, and where a lever
balances. Secondary
dependent variables were the three-way heaviness judgment (a manipulation check that the
cue was perceived at all), predictions in the scenes, task completion time, lever placement
error, and the presence score (the mean of four seven-point items), with the fifth item
reported separately as comfort.

We tested three hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1:} the gain in prediction accuracy from pre-test to post-test was larger
        with the cue on than with the cue off.
  \item \textbf{H2:} participants chose the heavier block more often with the cue on than with
        the cue off, where they would mostly report that the blocks felt the same (the
        manipulation check).
  \item \textbf{H3:} reported presence was higher with the cue on.
\end{itemize}
H1 was tested with a $2\times2$ mixed ANOVA on accuracy (cue, between subjects, $\times$
pre/post, within subjects), with the interaction term as the effect of interest; H2 with a
Mann-Whitney $U$ test on the number of correct choices per participant; and H3 with an
independent-samples $t$-test on the presence score.

\section{Status and Remaining Work}
\label{sec:deliverables}
By the mid-point demonstration in early November, the following were complete: a Unity~6
scene for the Quest~2 with grab-and-place for four materials, a lever, a water tank, and a
ramp, with pseudo-haptic weight modulation; scripted physics-fidelity checks against closed
forms for density, buoyancy, friction, and the lever; and an experiment mode that took a
participant ID, assigned the cue setting, ran the training, the trials, and the
questions, and logged all of it to file.

Remaining at that point were: at least one more concept built on the same framework (for
example a pulley or projectile launcher); recruiting and running the twelve-participant study
and analyzing it as described; a recorded demonstration of each concept; and the paper
extending this one, documenting the design, the system and human evaluation, and the
agentic-AI development workflow, grounded in the day-one development log.

\textbf{Roles:} This was a solo project (group CS567\_SVEN). Sven Skoglund was responsible for
all work, including the Unity interaction and pseudo-haptics, the physics-fidelity checks, the
demonstration, and directing the agentic-AI development workflow.

\section{Conclusion}
We built a VR physics sandbox that turned abstract relationships, from density and friction to
the mechanics of the lever, into things a learner could feel and test through direct, embodied
manipulation. The application was pure VR with no in-scene agent; agentic AI was the workflow
used to build it. Its validation showed the simulation matching the underlying physics, and its
experiment mode made the between-subjects study of the weight cue ready to run.
